Approximately 12 000 years ago, the United Planetary Assembly 
of the Vitalitaz Civilization (UPAVC)—the interplanetary 
government of the Vitalitaz, a civilization on the verge of 
attaining Type 2 status on the Kardashev scale and poised 
to wield full control over their solar systems energy—declared 
it was time to expand their influence beyond their home star system.



Their solar system, named the Vigilaniz System after the word 
“vigilance,” was protected by system-wide defenses developed 
over millennia. These shields guarded against external threats 
such as space debris and galactic cosmic radiation (GCR), as 
their star’s magnetic field was insufficient to protect 
electronics at the system's outer edges.



This new expansion mirrored the earlier phases of Vitalitaz history, 
when the precursor civilizations that would later form the UPAVC 
had begun colonizing celestial bodies beyond their home planet 
even before achieving Type 1 civilization status.



The question remained: how would they expand beyond their 
system? A committee assembled by the UPAVC evaluated 
numerous unconventional travel methods—from the still-theoretical 
wormholes and volatile antimatter engines to cryosleep travel 
and atomic disassembly-reassembly. After considerable 
deliberation, they formally proposed solar sails.



The proposal advanced with surprising speed, especially given 
the Assembly’s entrenched bureaucracy—a system largely 
unchanged since it had successfully stabilized relations between 
the various interplanetary civilizations and unified them under the UPAVC.



After the proposal was approved, an agency was hastily formed: 
The Solar Agency for Making Solar-Ray Sails for Travel Outside 
he Vigilaniz System (TSASRSTVS). It quickly publicized and 
executed an unprecedented plan.



Solar sails—light in mass but vast in area—are propelled by focused 
light beams. When accelerated by beams originating within the solar 
system, a sail can travel only one way. However, if the beams are 
projected from outside the system, the sail could, in theory, be 
slowed or recalled if unforeseen dangers arose during the voyage. 
Deploying such external beams would require sending an advance 
fleet of “beacon sails” beyond the system—a feasible sacrifice, 
given the vast material reserves of the asteroid belt Noctic Fractum.



These beacon sails would be equipped with relativistic navigation systems. 
Stationed along the planned route, they would fire corrective beams if they 
detected hazardous space debris too small to be seen from home. 
Even if destroyed, their final telemetry would map the debris, 
refining the charts for future voyages.



But before such an intricate network could be built, agency engineers 
first needed to master sail propulsion itself. Using Procedural Inventor 
Machines (PIM)—a branch of systems descended from AEIs (Artificial 
Emissary Intelligences), expert-semigeneralist intellects that seemingly 
surpassed even the brightest Vitalizers in their tasks yet serving as 
emissaries, they rapidly developed simple prototypes. These early sails, 
pushed by fusion-powered hyperluminous beams, 
soon enabled swift travel between planets 
at a significant fraction of light speed.



Relativity, once an abstract theory from a pre-planetary age, suddenly 
became intuitive. At such velocities, the immutable cosmic speed limit 
for matter made strange phenomena measurable and coherent:

- Length contraction, which effectively shortens the travel distance.

- Time dilation, slowing time for the moving traveler, whether 
from velocity or gravity.

- Relativistic Doppler shift, blueshifting light from approaching 
objects and redshifting it from receding ones.

- Terrell rotation, the visual distortion of objects passed at 
near-light speed.

- Differential aging, a consequence of acceleration-induced time dilation 
between travelers and those who remain behind.



Given their near-Type 2 status, one might assume the 
Vitalitaz had conquered biological aging—the final step in overcoming 
the limitations that end life and individual conscious experience. 
Yet they had not. One reason was the compressed timeline of 
their ascent; their solar system was compact, accelerating their
journey from a Type 1 to a verge-of-Type 2 civilization. This left 
little time for the profound, rapid shifts in societal philosophy 
necessary to universally embrace immortality, especially while 
the dominant cultural energy was focused on the urgent project 
of solar system colonization.



Technically, biological immortality—or simply "immortality," as it came 
to be known—was within their reach and could have been made 
accessible to all. Such universal access would have been a political 
necessity to prevent civil unrest born of inequity. However, the 
prevailing belief system of the Vitalizers, unified under Thaliora, 
placed supreme value on urgency: the duty to contribute to civilization’s 
advancement in an efficient and meaningful manner. This cultural 
engine demanded the development of strong, altruistic character 
capable of wielding advanced technology responsibly, urgency 
being seen as a necesarry fuel for this development.



Yet urgency was a double-edged sword. While it drove monumental 
progress, it also cultivated profound psychological strain. Urgency 
sometimes imposed heavy burdens; burdens sometimes bred stress; 
and stress sometimes clouded the mind. A clouded mind usually lost 
self-control, leading to breakdowns in the face of resistance. These 
breakdowns could crystallize into permanent, hellish states: anhedonia, 
existential fragility, chronic indecision, and worse—states that 
incentivized rejecting reality in favor of comforting delusions.



The Vitalitaz civilization understood this duality intimately, along with 
other necessary but perilous mental landscapes, too complex to detail 
here. Over millennia, they refined Thaliora through a combination of 
intuitive revelation—some claimed to have received messages from 
the celesitals themselves—and deliberate philosophical effort, 
systematizing these insights into a coherent worldview. It provided 
balanced guidance for navigating the mind’s most treacherous 
territories, a framework considered indispensable even in their 
advanced age, where base emotional states could be bought 
and sold as commodities.



Within the framework of Thaliora, immortality was interpreted 
as a potential catalyst for isolation and passivity. It could supplant 
the inner drive to fulfill one’s purpose with a mere defense against
other forms of death —a defense that, in itself, offered no ultimate 
protection. Such a shift would radically alter the social and power 
dynamics necessary to sustain civilization, a transformation 
feared to risk the universe’s only known chance at self-understanding, 
given the Great Filters the Vitalitaz had already surmounted.



Thus, from this interpretation, a broad consensus emerged 
among the Vitalizers:

- Death-awareness is necessary for the advancement of civilization.

And its inevitable corollary:

- Immortality catalyzes civilization's collapse.



Yet even its adherents acknowledged that death was merely a means, 
not an end. They understood its profound suffering—for the individual 
facing oblivion and for those left behind, witnessing an individual 
conscious experience extinguished forever, a companion lost 
beyond all reunion.



Yet a secretive minority questioned the very premises of this consensus. 
From their ranks, a significant faction developed a divergent philosophy 
concerning life, death, immortality, and civilization’s trajectory. 
Because of they developed an alternative philosophy, they 
called themselves The Divergers.



The Divergers identified the two core premises underpinning 
the societal consensus:

- Death-awareness causes urgency.

- Urgency is necessary for advancing civilization.



Few Vitalizers—and The Divergers, while a faction, still considered 
themselves Vitalizers—disputed the second premise in its entirety, 
though they debated the degree of urgency required. Their 
fundamental dissent lay with the first premise. For The Divergers, 
what made existence precious was life itself: consciousness and 
the singular cascade of experiences it enabled—experiences of 
gratitude, fostered by centuries of civilizational harmony; the joy 
of creative craft; the awe of encountering the never-before-seen; 
and the profound abilities that set them apart from the inanimate 
and primitive living world. (Their only potential rivals, the AGEIs, 
remained dutiful emissaries.)



This was the desired essence of being. Why, then, would they 
choose to end it when the theoretical means to extend life 
indefinitely lay within their grasp? Even if life after death 
was a phenomenon, the chances of being reborn as a 
Vitalizer remained close to zero.



The central question remained: how could one embrace such 
boundless life without falling into the trap of wastefulness—without 
proving the correctness of first premise of the consensus 
they sought to challenge? How does a civilization live 
forever without ceasing to live?



The Divergers studied the masters and grandmasters of history, 
finding that many retained their intellectual sharpness until their 
final days. This was because they had discovered their unique 
aptitudes and inclinations—their "superpowers"—which allowed 
them to advance collective understanding in ways they found 
deeply meaningful. For them, the process itself, the flow-like 
state of immersive craft, was reason enough to continue, 
regardless of age. Many lamented that a single lifetime 
was insufficient to implement all the ideas they envisioned.



Given this, why would such innate eagerness fade under 
immortality? Modern Vitalizers had exponentially more 
opportunities than their ancestors, enough that just about 
anyone could master their chosen craft without becoming 
a hindrance to others.



The Divergers believed immortality would be essential for 
ascending to a Type 3 civilization. Age cultivates ingrained 
syntheses of experience, wisdom, efficient intuitions, deep 
abstractions, and practical judgment. The gap between Type 2 
and Type 3 was unfathomably vast; beings with millennia of 
accumulated insight would provide a far more stable foundation 
for such a transition than mortal beings, who would have to 
relearn civilization’s complexity from scratch with each generation.



This was the Divergers’ prognosis, based on their civilization’s 
advanced understanding—though they acknowledged the 
uncertainty of what unexplainable mental states future 
societies might develop.



Ultimately, The Divergers viewed death as an unnecessary 
truncation of experience. They would not willingly surrender 
life if given the choice. Yet the UPAVC, guided by its 
interpretation of Thaliora, seemed poised to do exactly that.



The Divergers might have tolerated this ideological divide if 
the government had remained pragmatically adaptable. But 
over recent decades, the UPAVC had grown increasingly rigid,
failing to evolve its philosophy in step with the very technological 
progress its very reign enabled. Worse, its power had become 
so absolute that The Divergers feared its interpretation of reality 
was hardening into reality itself. Any reality contradicting 
UPAVC and the Thaliora could now be suppressed rather than 
examined, converting Thaloria from a guide into a dogma. Even 
means to an end—like mortality as a driver of progress—risked 
becoming unquestioned ends in themselves.



This fear was crystallized in a specific incident: when 
Diverger-aligned scientists proposed doubling the Vitalizer 
average lifespan from 230 to 460 years—including neurotransmitter 
enhancements to sustain motivation—a project the UPAVC should 
logically have supported, it was abruptly canceled under the 
pretext of "respecting Thaliora." The Divergers suspected the 
true motive was ideological preservation: such a change might 
alter the societal consensus on mortality, potentially 
destabilizing the very foundation of UPAVC’s power.



The pattern became undeniable, as the Divergers got denied 
every time they proposed unconventional life-extension projects. 
On another occasion, Diverger scientists sought funding to 
develop immortal microscopic lifeforms—a foundational step 
toward broader applications. After months of unnecessary 
bureaucratic delays, the proposal was formally rejected. 
It was now clear: the UPAVC would not foster any competency 
in life-extension technology, even as The Divergers felt the 
moment for such breakthroughs had never been more ripe.



Trapped within a cage of their own civilization's making, they 
pondered escape. Fleeing to a neighboring star system to found 
a new society was a theoretical alternative, but without rapid 
engines and under the UPAVC's undisputed authority, such a 
journey was impossible. Transcending their biological form—a 
concept the AGEIs had the computational capacity to realize—was 
a forbidden path. Thaliora's strictures were encoded into their 
very governance protocols, preventing the creation of such 
transcendent forms. Frustrated, they temporarily shelved 
these grand plans, focusing instead on subtler philosophical 
divergences within Thaliora's own interpretive framework.



During this period of strategic inactivity, an astronomer named 
Serendipius pursued a personal project: cataloging the temporal 
changes of celestial bodies. Over the past two centuries, the 
Vitalitaz had mapped trillions of worlds using the Ocular Array—a 
network of ultra-precise, adaptive mirrors capable of resolving 
surface details on planets millions of light-years away, discerning 
features as fine as the pores on a rock.



Serendipius employed image-recognition algorithms to identify 
which bodies had changed the most—and, curiously, the least—over 
the observation period. It was a endeavor of pure curiosity. To hians 
surprise, among the most static objects in the cosmos were not 
dead rocks or cold giants, but living planets—worlds entirely 
blanketed in dense, plantoid biospheres.



The finding was shocking. Life, by its nature, alters its environment; 
the rapid terraforming of the Vigilaniz System was the prime example. 
Yet here, evolution appeared to have halted. Competition, adaptation, 
change—all were absent. These worlds were perfectly still.



Serendipius devoted every resource to the anomaly. After exhaustive 
analysis, out-of-the-box reasoning, and consultation with the few 
trusted colleagues, only one conclusion remained. It was a revelation 
so profound that its truth left Serendipius in a state of sustained, 
otherworldly awe for at least ten standard days:




                 The plantoids were immortal.




The plantoids were immortal. 
They existed in the most harmonious 
ecosystems ever observed, drawing balanced nutrition from their 
surroundings—sufficient for all, yet insufficient to incentivize 
reproduction. Evolution on these worlds appeared to have reached a 
final, static equilibrium; the lifeforms seemed content, with no 
drive to procreate or compete.



Here, then, was immortal life. It was idle, perhaps, but it thrived 
in harmonious symbiosis with its inanimate environment. It was living 
proof that immortality’s benefits could outweigh its 
perceived downsides.



Once the initial shock subsided, Serendipius shared the findings 
with fellow Divergers and proposed a deeper inquiry: were there 
other immortal biospheres hidden in the vast catalog of observed 
worlds? Using AGEIs to re-analyze the data, unmonitored, against 
the template of the plantoid planets, they discovered a handful of 
similar worlds. One was extraordinary: a planet hosting not just 
plantoids, but also animoids and exoids. Despite their primitive 
and varied biology, all lived in perfect, immortal 
harmony—evolution’s game seemingly satisfied with its final state.



Remarkably, on this world, immortality did not equate to stagnation. 
In the absence of death and deadly competition, activity and 
creativity persisted:

- Drill-nosed creatures bored complex tunnels, triggering 
volcanic vents that evaporated water from salt-rich rocks.

- Ring-shaped plants captured and stored wind energy.

- Living vines bound together floating islands in the atmosphere.

- Half-spheroid beings floated among the clouds, sculpting their forms.

Here was the ultimate proof: life could be both immortal and 
dynamically creative, shaping its world without the 
existential goad of mortality.



The discovery presented the Divergers with a profound dilemma. 
What could they do with this information? The UPAVC, thankfully 
founded on principles of individual privacy and intellectual 
liberty—rights deemed essential for unrestricted thought—did 
not employ mass surveillance. Yet, given the government’s 
recent ideological rigidity, publicly presenting the evidence 
seemed perilous. They risked seeing their discovery co-opted; 
the UPAVC could easily argue that these immortal 
lifeforms were merely primitive, fragile Type 0.5 biospheres, 
stagnant and vulnerable to planetary catastrophes. To 
compare such simple, planet-bound harmony to the 
dynamic, solar-system-spanning ambition of the Vitalitaz 
would be a false equivalence—like equating the frequent, 
favorable mutations in microbes with the more stable genetic 
frameworks necessary for complex, multicellular life.
What would happen after that, the divergers didn't want to
discover.



The truth was too risky to share. For now, it had to remain a secret.



Faced with this situation, The Divergers devised a flexible, multi-layered 
plan to mitigate their vulnerability and advance their goals. The choice 
was clear: they could not share the secret, and they could not stay. 
Their objective was therefore audacious: they would voyage to one of 
the very solar systems harboring immortal life, using the cover of a false 
crisis to execute an exodus, and discover themselves
what made life immortal.



The operation unfolded in sequential, but sometimes overlapping stages:

- Infiltration: Diverger agents patiently embedded themselves 
within key bureaucratic and scientific bodies.

- The Catalyst: After seven years, they activated a disinformation 
campaign, warning of a dangerously close star on the verge of a 
supernova. The only viable defense, they argued, was a fleet of 
advanced solar sails—a mission that could be publicly framed as 
a grand exploration to maintain morale.

- Institutional Leverage: The manufactured crisis spurred the creation 
of a new agency for rapid solar sail development. Within two years, 
sails were being mass-produced and made widely available.

- The Exodus: Through careful bureaucratic navigation, Divergers 
secured ships fitted with stealth surfaces and materials designed 
to evade the Vigilaniz System’s formidable defenses. Then, they 
sailed away, vanishing into the void.



Their hope was dual-fold: to never be seen again, or to one day 
return as "Revitalizers"—bearers of the ultimate proof of harmonious 
immortality. They would present this discovery not as a rebellion, 
but as a fulfillment of Thaliora, catalyzing a peaceful evolution in 
conviction. In their ideal future, the UPAVC would integrate this 
truth and continue its reign, transforming their civilization into 
a true paradise, perfected and eternal.

You said 12 000 years? From the reference frame of our home system, 
light takes about 11 995 years to travel from Vigilaniz to where we are 
now—a place we’ve named the Suehtemorp System. But for us, it 
has been only eight. Length contraction is not a theory here; it folded 
the void, squeezing the distance between one impossibility and another.



Our destination was here: a planet enshrined in immortal life. Yet we 
could not land. Why? Despite being on the verge of Type 2, we have 
no mathematics for this. The most descriptive word is "magic". The 
visual distortions defy Terrell rotation, violate every projection. 
And the debris… shards of ships that look like our own, but broken 
apart in ways we don’t build—they evaporate like phantom fireworks 
the moment they cross a threshold equal to the thrice the planet’s 
minimum radius. How did they get here? Were other Vitalizers 
here before us? Or is this another of the system’s… magic tricks?



Because nothing here obeys. The star shifts color and intensity, 
sometimes vanishing into pitch black, seeming to rotate freely in 
a space that has no up. An atmospheric glow permeates the 
vacuum, far from any planet. Celestial bodies blink in and out 
of existence as if sliding through a fourth spatial dimension.



We landed on a neighboring world, seeking solid ground. 
We found only deeper anomaly.



It was a landscape of hallucination: glowing hexagons, halos, 
and cubes, their interiors filled with color-shifting metallic bricks. 
The ground itself refused to stay fixed—one moment grass, 
the next a tessellation of tree-floor, then cylinders and toruses 
orbiting, void? Tubular and circular gates hung in the air, 
radiating streams of unknown particles.



We sought immortality to extend experience. To be forever ready 
for vividness. But I fear a darker truth: that after we grow 
familiar with these wonders, they will cease to be anomalies. 
They will become a permanent, sterile backdrop. And the
most terrifying void is not the emptiness of space, but 
the exhaustion of wonder itself. But perhaps I'm too 
restrictive in my perception? 



Why am I talking to you as if we haven’t already reached the 
source of immortality? Because getting through this… 
place… 
hasn’t been simple.



Let me break it down.



If you try to cross one of the glowing shapes—unless it’s red—you 
get instantly teleported back to the landing site. The red ones let 
you pass. The circular gates set your velocity to a fixed value. 
The tubular gates seem inert… unless you’ve picked up an 
‘armament’ from the ground and stored it in your portable material 
compressor. Then, they either equip or de-equip it from your hands.



And these ‘armaments’… well, you hold them. Unsharp swords, 
lazer guns, and... Ice creams? They can destroy the glowing 
‘targets’—all except the red ones. You’d think that would 
solve everything.



It doesn’t.



Make a wrong move—and you’ll know, because the ‘magic’ zaps 
you back—and the entire arena resets. Targets reassemble. It’s 
like the invisible architecture of this place, these… fields, ghosts, 
rules… demands you perform a specific sequence of actions. 
A ritual of friendliness? A test of pattern?



Why not just blast through with our own tech? Please. We’re in a 
solar system that constantly makes you question if you’ve 
accidentally ingested hallucinogens. Flying home? Impossible. 
Whatever cages us here makes UPAVC’s security look like a 
child’s lock.



So yes, we’re trapped. Our whole entourage—tens of us—is 
scattered. Some, driven by excitement united with 
sheer choicelessness, have ventured deeper. 
Towards what we call 
The Treacherous Terrestials. 
We haven’t heard from them, not even via telepathic 
link. 
They’re just… gone.



But look, the nutrition synthesizers work fine. We won’t starve. 
And let’s not forget the mission: discover the source of immortality, 
learn its secrets, implement it, and return home as Revitalizers 
to change our world for the better!



I am Lucis! Nice to meet you! I'm exited to acompany you 
along this journey!







Now, let’s go find it!
